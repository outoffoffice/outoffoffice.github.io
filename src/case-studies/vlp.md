## 1. Challenge

Finding a topic for your bachelor thesis is always harder than it sounds. One sentence helped me get there — something someone once told me: choose something you wouldn't just want to check off after handing it in, but would actually want to keep pursuing. That narrowed the search, away from a pure obligation and toward a question: how can digital music be made tangible and haptic again?

Sharing music used to mean putting together a mixtape: choosing songs, thinking through the order, designing your own cover — because you knew the cassette would end up sitting on someone's shelf. Streaming has left only the song selection intact. There's no object left to hold, no sleeve to design, no artwork beyond a small playlist cover you barely notice while scrolling. Music has stopped being tangible, in the literal sense.

The idea at the start was to build a dedicated player for it — a physical device for digital playlists. A year and a half later, Senic released almost exactly that with the Muse Player, a good sign the direction was right. Still, I deliberately decided against it. A standalone device would have set the bar too high, more suited to niche enthusiasts than everyday use. What I was after was the opposite: a solution that works instantly, without extra hardware, that catches on as fast as it's used.

## 2. Concept: Key Instead of Storage Medium

A CD or cassette stores the music itself. The tag doesn't — it only stores access. That decision was central: instead of inventing a new format to compete with streaming, the tag becomes a physical key to the libraries that already exist. You don't get an object with limited content, but an object that points to a theoretically unlimited selection of songs while still staying put on a shelf.

## 3. Decisions + Process: Curators Instead of Algorithm

Spotify calls its auto-generated playlists "Your Mixtape." The algorithm learns from your own listening habits, but there's no real person with a personal concept behind it. A mixtape only gains its value through the person who put it together — their choices, their view of their own music. That's why two real curators each put together a playlist for VLP, each with its own character, its own handwriting. Not a data-driven suggestion, but a selection made by one person for another.

## 4. Technical Execution: A QR Code That Feels Different Than It Looks

A QR code printed on paper would have been the simplest solution. Instead, the code itself is printed in relief, not printed onto the surface: a dark base layer, topped with a light layer in the shape of the code, so it stands out from its background both visually and to the touch. A detail no one needs for scanning, but one that's immediately noticeable when you touch it. That's exactly where the difference from a plain printout was meant to lie.
