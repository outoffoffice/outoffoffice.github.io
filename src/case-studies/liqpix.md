## 1. Challenge

A treatment room is not a neutral space. It's where bodies are examined, interpreted, brought back into order. The image hanging in it has to relate to that context without illustrating it. The brief sounded simple at first: a large-scale, atmosphere-defining image for the treatment room of a physiotherapy/osteopathy practice, with one concrete constraint. The color world should sit in blues and turquoises, likely chosen for their calming effect, fitting for a space centered on treatment and recovery. Underneath that seemingly simple task, though, sat two requirements that quietly work against each other.

On one side, it needed to be a self-sufficient, abstract image, something that works as a piece in its own right, not decoration with a message attached. On the other, it had to belong in this exact room, one concerned with the body, tissue, structure, and repair. Pushed too far into abstraction, it risks becoming arbitrary: a nice image that could hang anywhere. Pushed too far into the literal, stylized anatomy, muscle cross-sections, textbook aesthetics, it turns clinical, cold, redundant to what already happens in the room.

The real challenge, then, wasn't stylistic. It was structural: how do you generate visual meaning not through depiction, but through the principle an image is built on?

## 2. My Role

Concept, design, and technical image production sat in one hand. No brief handed off to be executed elsewhere, but one continuous process from first idea through pixel-level execution. This position was necessary: the core idea of the piece doesn't emerge in a concept phase and then get "produced." The execution *is* the idea. Thinking and craft can't be separated here without losing both.

## 3. Decisions + Process

**Against depiction, for principle.** The obvious solution for an image in a treatment room would have been anatomy: stylized muscles, fiber patterns, a body recognizable as a body. The decision went the other way. Not to *depict* what tissue looks like, but to *trace* how it forms. No anatomical reference, but a building principle instead: smallest unit assembling into structure, structure assembling into wholeness. The image needed to feel like tissue without showing any.

**Pixels as the smallest working unit.** From that follows the second decision. If the principle matters more than the picture, the image logic itself has to follow that principle too. The pixel becomes the conceptual equivalent of a cell or fiber: the smallest unit, meaningless on its own, that becomes structure through grouping and repetition. This wasn't a stylistic choice so much as a decision about which level of the image to actually work on. Not shapes or areas, but the individual points that everything else is built from.

**The line between structure and noise.** The third, hardest decision wasn't a single one. It repeated constantly. When does an area start to read as tissue, organic, directional, almost biologically plausible, and when does it tip back into mere noise? There's no formula for that, no threshold value. That line could only be found by looking, over and over, iteration after iteration. This decision is exactly what turns into method in the next section.

## 4. Technical Execution

Even though there are countless other ways to produce an artwork like this through shader or scripts the process happens directly on screen, filter by filter, look by look. Still, it follows a logic that breaks down into four recurring moves.

**Seeding.** The starting point is noise: no shape, no direction, only potential. Just as tissue in the body doesn't emerge from a pre-drawn blueprint but from a collection of smallest, initially directionless units, the image begins without a compositional plan. Just pixels that don't want to be anything yet.

**Directional Growth.** Photoshop's Wind filter, originally built for motion-blur effects, gets repurposed here. It forces a direction onto the noise. Much like fiber structures in tissue organize along lines of tension, the pixel field picks up a first preferred direction. Not drawn, but imposed.

**Diffusion.** Smearing, blurring, softening transitions. There are no hard edges between tissue types in the body, a transition is always gradual. The Distort process takes on that function here, translating hard contrasts into gradients, until the image stops looking "edited" and starts looking "grown."

**Clustering.** The decisive moment isn't any single filter. It's the look in between. When do individual areas start to read as something that resembles structure, rather than noise? This observation can't be automated. It's the actual creative act in this process.

These four steps don't run strictly in sequence. They loop, sometimes repeating, sometimes reordering, depending on what the observation calls for in the moment. Action, observation, correction, repetition. No predetermined outcome, but an iterative, response-based self-organization, where every iteration reacts to the one before it. Not unlike how biological tissue doesn't form according to a plan, but through the interplay of local reactions.

Technically, this principle could well be rebuilt procedurally. Reaction-diffusion systems, for instance, generate algorithmically very similar organic structures. The deliberate choice here was still the manual route, not for lack of an alternative, but because it allows every intermediate step to be seen, judged, and corrected. A degree of control a running algorithm doesn't offer.
